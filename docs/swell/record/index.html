Science

The three ideas behind SWELL

The project's design rule: chemistry does the resolution work, so the optics can stay modest. More expansion with a modest lens is meant to stand in for expensive glass.

1 · Expand the sample instead of buying better lenses

A lens cannot separate two points closer than about 0.61·λ/NA. For the 0.65 NA objective at 520 nm that is 488 nm. Expansion moves the molecules apart before the light ever reaches the lens, and the project's rule of thumb is effective resolution ≈ optical resolution ÷ E. In original-specimen units that is about 108 nm at 4.5×, and 79 nm at 10× with the 0.40 NA objective (optics alone: that track's camera samples at 0.84× Nyquist, a little coarser than the lens resolves, so detail near this limit can alias).

The lens's diffraction limit does not change; the specimen gets bigger. These are paper numbers, and still not electron microscopy: you only see what you label, and the chemistry can make its own artefacts.

Who got there first: Zhang, Chang and colleagues paired expansion with a webcam mini-microscope in 2016 ("expansion mini-microscopy"), reaching, after expansion, a resolution comparable to a benchtop microscope's on unexpanded samples, at a hundredth to a thousandth of the price. SWELL builds on their idea.

2 · The muscle's stripes as a built-in ruler

E = SLpost ÷ SLpre

Sarcomere spacing is measured on the same myofibril before and after expansion. The project's notes call the period blur-invariant: blur softens each stripe but leaves the spacing alone, so the ratio survives modest optics. The before-image should need no label: differential phase contrast with four oblique LEDs is predicted to show the stripes (the project's choice after the physics check; untested), with crossed polarisers as a cross-check. That method builds on asymmetric-illumination differential phase contrast as Mehta and Sheppard described it (2009), and on the quantitative, multi-angle form Tian and Waller showed on an LED array microscope (2015).

It replaced the textbook A-band ruler, which needs sarcomeres long enough to show an I-band: only about 13% of supermarket breast's sarcomeres at 4×, computed for a mean length of 1.69 µm with an assumed, not measured, spread of 0.08 µm (standard deviation).

Who got there first: Chen, Tillberg and Boyden (2015), who introduced expansion microscopy, imaged the same cells before and after expansion and measured how far lengths moved (under 1% of the distance in cultured cells, 2–4% in brain slices). Sheard and colleagues (2019) used the periodicity of sarcomeres and the fixed width of Z-discs as intrinsic reporters of local gel expansion in heart cells, and Sheard's 2021 thesis found the sarcomere factor lower than the gel's. A 2022 thesis by Migliore expanded isolated rabbit myofibrils, imaged them without labels and used the A-band as the ruler. VISTA (Miao, Lin, Qian and Wei, 2022) measured expansion by comparing label-free images of the same structures before and after. In our dated searches (docs/design/prior_art.md in the download, 10 October 2026) we found nothing that pairs each myofibril's label-free period before expansion with its own period after.

3 · Ordinary optics, chosen to be just good enough

Motic PL 40X/0.65 and PL 20X/0.40 plan objectives, one 100 mm achromat 25 mm behind the objective, and a mono IMX296 global-shutter camera (1456 × 1088 px, 3.45 µm) at £49.87. With the 40X objective, for the 4× track: 17.8× and 0.194 µm pixels, 1.03× Nyquist. With the 20X objective, for the 10× track: 8.9× and 0.388 µm pixels, 0.84× Nyquist.

The stock OpenFlexure lens would undersample (0.66× Nyquist), and a colour sensor wastes signal, so neither is used. The stage would be a fork of the open OpenFlexure Microscope v7, run by a Raspberry Pi 5 and a Pico 2.

Built on open work: the OpenFlexure Microscope (Collins et al., 2020; build docs), expansion microscopy (Chen, Tillberg & Boyden, 2015), expansion mini-microscopy (Zhang, Chang et al., 2016), sarcomeres as intrinsic expansion reporters (Sheard et al., 2019), U-ExM (Gambarotto et al., 2019), pan-ExM (M'Saad & Bewersdorf, 2020), TREx (Damstra et al., 2022) and differential phase contrast (Mehta & Sheppard, 2009; Tian & Waller, 2015). Every source, with what SWELL took from it, is listed under credits.

Results

What the simulation says, including where it fails

Every result here comes from synthetic gels with a known truth. Synthetic data tests the analysis pipeline against our own assumptions, not the chemistry: a real build would have to repeat every check on real gels.

Read this first. This section is version 1, measured on gels from our own generator. The only other test, a test gel built separately by a different AI agent, which may share our blind spots (see Tested harder) went worse: per-myofibril axial error 1.8% RMS with 7.5% beyond ±2%, a transverse bias of −17.8%, and 0% of the under-expanded patch flagged. Treat everything below as development results.

The first estimate was too optimistic

simulated

The first desk calculation said one myofibril segment of 10 sarcomeres gives its expansion to 0.21–0.35% (RMS), for assumed before-image contrast of 5–50%. It assumed perfectly regular sarcomeres. Real ones vary by about 3% from one to the next, and under that jitter (and at some sarcomere lengths) the original estimator (a free-frequency fit) locked onto side-lobes: 10–17% of myofibrils came out wrong by 7–19%. The first full whole-gel simulation failed 15 checks.

The fix is the Z-disc ladder: the Z-discs in the expanded image define a sarcomere-by-sarcomere coordinate, and the before-image is fitted on that ladder, so the jitter is carried along instead of fought. Segments need at least 8 sarcomeres. Result: 0.3–0.6% RMS per myofibril across simulation runs, 0.32–0.48% across the four gels of the final run and 0.35% on the blind gel, with at most 0.48% of myofibrils off by more than ±2%.

These precision figures hold only under the simulation's conditions: before-image stripes with a fundamental contrast of 10.5% at 5,000 detected photons per sample (q ≈ 7.4), shot noise only, a Gaussian blur of exactly known width, and one-dimensional profiles handed straight to the ruler, with no image to segment or stitch. The design's DPC before-image is predicted to reach only 2.3% contrast, made up for by about 200,000 detected photons per sample (q ≈ 10), again assuming shot noise only.

Our own blind gel (version 1, development)

Our blind gel was made by the same generator with a new random seed, every planted defect moved and resized, and nothing tuned after it ran. It is not independent, because our generator shares our assumptions; a test gel built separately by a different AI agent, which may share our blind spots, is the harder test (see Tested harder). It holds 1,200 myofibrils in a 1.5 mm punch expanded 4.5×.

Coverage: on our own gels (version 1), about half the myofibrils carry the axial ruler

Every myofibril gets the transverse (width) ruler and the landmark (position) ruler. Only segments of at least 8 sarcomeres get the axial period ratio: 665 of 1,200 (55%) in the blind gel and 626 of 1,200 (52%) in the development gels. In real meat this would depend on how gently the tissue is homogenised.

Transverse isotropy and the shape-aware ruler

A gel can swell evenly along a myofibril and unevenly across it. So the pass criteria include transverse isotropy, Eax ÷ Etr − 1 within ±3% (95% CI, n ≥ 200 myofibrils), and the axial and transverse factors are always reported separately. That interval counts only the scatter between myofibrils, not error they all share, and on our synthetic gels it has proved too narrow (Tested harder, point 3), so a real test would have to use whole gels as the unit; that test isn't designed yet. On the blind gel the transverse factor per myofibril is 0.00% ± 0.50% (bias ± SD).

The width ruler is the fragile one: it needs a model of each cross-section. A boxy cross-section fitted as an ellipse is off by −9.0% to −15.2%, depending on the before-image method. So the pipeline measures each myofibril's cross-section shape from the expanded z-stack, and the bias drops to ≤ 0.04%.

Blur calibration still matters. If the before-image blur is calibrated 5% too wide, the bias is 1.05–3.23%, so the first gate calibrates it to ±2%. At that target the worst bias in our own model is 1.09%. The test gel built separately by a different AI agent showed the model is not enough: a blur shape the ruler does not model biased it by −17.8%, with the right blur widths or not.

On a development gel, a planted 2% anisotropy is recovered as −2.12% [−2.37%, −1.87%] (truth −2.03%), and a planted gel stretch of 2.00% along 23° is found by the orientation test as 2.03% along 23°.

open problem

Not enough light in the worst case

Widefield optics see the whole thickness of an expanded myofibril, so out-of-focus light adds a background pedestal. At the lowest labelling simulated (0.5% of amines) on a 2 µm myofibril, the signal-to-noise ratio falls 12.4 → 5.6 against a target of 10. Reaching the target needs 3.2× the dose (exposure × irradiance), which costs bleaching. Before the pedestal was modelled, the first pass had given 12–42 across the labelling range.

Not solved. The named routes were a dose budget, deconvolution and a dye titration.

Tested harder

Weak spots found by a fresh check, and what changed

On 9 October 2026 the download was rerun from scratch by a check that had not built any of it: the fingerprint matched, all 11 tests passed and every number reproduced. Then the check listed what the tests could not show. Each point below is fixed, or stated as an open problem with its numbers. The first version was withdrawn on 10 October 2026, because it carried the original brief's superseded home-lab plan.

1 · A GEL BUILT SEPARATELY

A test gel we never saw

The old blind gel came from the same generator, and the pipeline was handed the before/after pairing. Now there is a test gel built separately by a different AI agent, which may share our blind spots. That agent read only the data format, never the detector code, and built in: lost fibrils, debris, a fold, a bubble, curved and bundled fibrils, tilted fibrils, expansion drifting during the scan, and its own blur and noise. The pipeline now matches the fibrils itself, and its result on that gel is published as the gel's builder scored it.

simulated What failed: the transverse ruler read widths about a fifth too small on average, which made the anisotropy call wrong in size (the sign was right). The cause is not the blur calibration: rerun with the true blur widths, the bias is still −17.4%. It is a blur shape the ruler does not model, and the pipeline's own landmark ruler already disagreed with it by −19.7% while the call was still made. Fixed afterwards (a development fix, since this gel has now been seen): no anisotropy call while the two rulers disagree by more than 3%. On this gel the call is now blocked. The ordinary under- and over-expanded patches, the curved fibrils and the tilted region went unflagged, and only about a quarter of the pairs got an axial ratio. The pairing, the debris rejection and the gel-level factor held. Like our own gels, this gel reached the pipeline as one set of profiles per object, cut out of its builder's images by an emulated image front-end, so SWELL's own code never segmented or stitched an image in these tests: read the pairing and debris scores as best cases for real images. The drift correction held too, but the slope in the table can't show it: the planted drift rose to +0.48% and fell back, so even with no correction its straight-line slope would have been only +0.07%/h, smaller than that slope's standard error. What shows it is that after the clock tile, the median error in each of six time bins stays within ±0.1%. The slope also leaves out 10 of its 320 myofibrils, those with errors beyond 5%; with them it reads −0.15%/h. The local expansion flag already uses the landmark ruler's area change as well as the axial ratio, but it fires only where the change, smoothed by a Gaussian of 45 µm standard deviation, exceeds 1%, or four standard errors where that is larger, and nothing in the under-expanded patch crossed that line. How a patch's size and strength decide whether it is caught has not been measured. These are open problems; this gel has now been seen, so each fix needs a new blind gel.

2 · THE BEFORE-IMAGE IS LOAD-BEARING

A go/no-go for the stripes

simulated The axial ruler is only as good as the label-free stripes before expansion. Its error follows one number, q = striation contrast × √photons: go if q ≥ 1.2 (1% per myofibril at 10 sarcomeres). An uneven light that the old code ignored no longer seems to matter: with a 20% slope the per-myofibril error was 0.95% with the old code and is 0.28% now, against 0.34% and 0.39% with even light. Each figure is the RMS of its own 300 random draws, not shared between cases, with no confidence interval, so small differences mean little: the new code's lower figure with a slope than without one should not be read as a gain. predicted From the physics of each mode, the choice is differential phase contrast with four oblique LEDs (predicted contrast 2.3%, q ≈ 10, assuming about 200,000 detected photons per sample and shot noise only). In one acquisition, crossed polarisers lose fibrils near 0° and 90°: only 42%–78% of orientations reach the go line. A second acquisition with the polariser pair turned 45° was not simulated.

3 · A −0.1% BIAS

Broken ends, and effect sizes instead of "significant"

simulated The ladder used the Z-discs at the very ends of each fragment, which are cut in half, and that pulled the axial factor low. They are now ignored. The gel-mean error on our own five development gels is now −0.08% to 0.00%, and the per-myofibril error 0.15%–0.18% (under the simulated conditions listed in the numbers). "The confidence interval excludes zero" had called a perfect gel anisotropic; now anisotropy counts only beyond ±1%. On the perfect gel the estimate is −0.10% [−0.18%, −0.03%], called isotropic; a planted 2% anisotropy reads −2.16% [−2.44%, −1.81%] (truth −1.98%). The brackets are 95% confidence intervals from resampling myofibrils, which treats them as independent and leaves out error they all share, such as the blur and cross-section model, so they are too narrow: they miss the true value on 4 of our 5 own gels, and on the test gel built separately by a different AI agent. The ±1% rule also hides a small bias: on our own gels the transverse ruler reads +0.05% to +0.14% high. A corrected interval (the bias removed, a term for shared error, its coverage checked by simulation, whole gels as the unit) is an open problem.

4 · WHEN THE BEFORE-IMAGE IS TAKEN

After the gel sets, and the gap measured

untested A protocol decision, never run. The protocol took the before-image before anchoring and gelling, so any change in sarcomere length in between would read as an expansion error. The reference image is now taken after the gel sets, on the rinsed gel, and the first three gels are imaged before anchoring too, to measure what that step does. The rinse is not settled either: the plan rinses the gel in PBS, a salt solution, and a sodium-acrylate gel may start to swell or buckle in it (reasoning, not a measurement), so the first gels must also show that the rinse leaves the gel's size unchanged, or the rinse must move to a liquid that does.

5 · ENOUGH LIGHT

How much dye the signal needs

For a signal-to-noise ratio of 10 at today's exposure, 0.81%–1.58% of the protein's amines must carry a dye (0.41%–0.79% at 200 ms). A pan-ExM-strength stain is estimated to label about 1.4% (range 0.30%–9.6%); the original recipe's weaker stain, about 0.14% (range 0.030%–0.96%), probably falls short: only the top of its range reaches what is needed. The model treats every amine in the protein as free to take a dye, but the anchoring step is expected to use some amines first, which it leaves out, so the real share could be lower. So the first gels titrate stronger stains. untested Still open until real gels measure it.

6 · THE GEL KEEPS MOVING

A clock tile for scan-time drift

simulated A gel can keep swelling during a two-hour scan, which would paint a fake gradient in the order the tiles were taken. One tile is now re-imaged every 50 tiles as a clock. On a synthetic gel drifting by 0.5%, the error trend falls from 0.22%/h to −0.036%/h (straight-line slopes, without standard errors: none were computed for this gel).

Still failing: on the blind gel, the gently stretched patch is flagged mostly by the wrong detector (40% by its own, 92% by any), and a 1.5 µm tear stays below the detection limit. Both are listed as open problems.

Evidence ladder

Cheapest tests first

Before anyone spends money on a lab, three home rungs, each done with an adult, test the parts of SWELL that chemistry can't fix: can the stripes be measured, are they bright enough to be a ruler, and are the fragments long enough? Each rung has a pass mark and a safety note.

RUNG 1

Measure a chicken with a laser

Shine a laser pointer through a thin strip of chicken breast and measure the diffraction spots: the sarcomere length falls out of one line of maths. How to do it.

Pass
first-order spots on both sides; three strips within ±5% of their mean, inside 1.5–2.0 µm
Safety
a family experiment, age guide 8 and up: the adult handles the laser, the knife, the glass and the raw chicken. Use a red (650 nm) Class 2 pointer, 1 mW or less: cheap green ones can leak invisible infrared. Never at eyes or shiny surfaces; lay the strip so the fibres run level, which keeps the side spots at table height; hold the slides in a peg or clip, not fingers; raw-meat hygiene

RUNG 2

See the stripes

Blend a pea-sized piece of chicken breast for a few seconds in ice-cold, weak salt water (6 g of salt per litre; much stronger brine dissolves the stripes), put a drop under a coverslip on any microscope with a 40× objective, and let swell-ruler measure the stripes. Plain bright-field with the condenser wide open shows almost nothing: close the condenser iris or tilt the lamp's light (never sunlight). Measure your pixel size with a stage micrometer (or from your rung-1 sarcomere length). No stain and no lab chemicals.

Pass
median q of 1.2 or more (rescaled to 0.194 µm pixels; use raw frames, ideally from a mono camera: a colour camera's demosaicing and any denoising or JPEG compression smooth the noise and make q read too high; sharpening changes it too; saving such a frame as PNG doesn't undo that) over 10+ myofibrils with 10+ sarcomeres: SWELL's go/no-go for the label-free ruler. Bright-field in salt water is not the design's DPC in a gel, so a pass is encouraging, not proof; and q is read from the image's own noise, so a check that doesn't depend on it, measuring each fibril's period twice (two frames, or its two halves), is still to be built
Safety
age guide 10 and up, with an adult. Use the microscope's own lamp: never light it with sunlight or point its mirror at the sun. Kitchen salt only. The adult handles the raw chicken, runs the blender, and handles the coverslip, the glass slides and the stage micrometer; children look and run the software. Raw-meat hygiene

RUNG 3

Long enough fragments

The ruler needs fragments with at least 8 sarcomeres. The same images give a length histogram; gentler blending keeps fragments longer.

Pass
40% or more of fragments with 8+ sarcomeres (the simulation assumed 44%)
Safety
as rung 2

RUNG 4 · LAB ONLY

First gels in a proper lab

Only once rungs 1–3 pass, and only in a lab with a fume hood, under its own safety rules. The gel chemistry uses restricted carcinogenic reagents. Never at home.

Pass
3 gels: pre-image q ≥ 1.2; per-myofibril axial precision ≤ 1% (per_fibril_spread in result.json); isotropy inside the ±3% gate (how to judge it is open: one gel's interval over its myofibrils has proved too narrow on our synthetic gels, so the gels themselves should be the unit). Reported, not a pass mark: the gel-mean axial factor next to the overview camera's whole-gel factor (the gel's diameter after ÷ before), and their ratio. A gate of ±0.5% on that ratio was dropped on 10 October 2026, before anyone climbed: a whole gel's factor is itself uncertain (observers' estimates of one gel ranged from 8.6 to 9.4 in GelMap, Damstra et al. 2023), and a myofibril may expand less than its gel.

Where the chemistry happens

Finding a suitable lab, anywhere

The gel chemistry belongs only in an equipped, supervised laboratory that works under its own safety rules. We name no labs here: this is the list of what to ask one for. Nobody has been contacted about SWELL.

  1. A fume hood, or local exhaust ventilation, for weighing powders and for the formaldehyde and monomer steps.
  2. Written permission for these reagents: acrylamide, bis-acrylamide and formaldehyde (CMR substances), APS, TEMED, sodium acrylate, imidazole, an SDS buffer at 95 °C and NHS-ester dyes in DMSO.
  3. A hazardous-waste route for liquids and polymerised gels, and what it costs.
  4. An ordering route: the lab orders the CMR reagents, because suppliers may not sell them to private individuals. Never present yourself as a business if you are not one.
  5. Lockable chemical storage, and fridge and freezer space for reagents and gels.
  6. Basic equipment: pipettes, a mini-centrifuge, a heat block reaching 95 °C, an incubator, a balance and a pH meter.
  7. Bench space for a small low-voltage microscope for a few months, under the lab's own induction, risk assessments and lone-working rules.
  8. Then choose: among the labs that confirm the first four points, the cheapest that can start when you need it.

Safety

The chemistry is the dangerous part

Wet work only in a proper lab with a fume hood. Nothing chemical at home.

Several reagents are carcinogens, mutagens or reproductive toxicants (CMR substances). Not in a kitchen, a garage or a spare room. Follow the host lab's induction and risk assessments. The law (UK, as the project's notes read it)

UK REACH Annex XVII (entries 28–30) means CMR substances can't be supplied to the general public. This covers acrylamide and, by Reg. (EU) 2018/675 as retained in UK law, formaldehyde. This law doesn't restrict the instrument itself, but the chemistry needs professional-user access, and it belongs only in an equipped, supervised laboratory working under its own safety rules. Never misrepresent yourself to a supplier.

Hazardous waste goes through a licensed route with records; nothing hazardous goes down the drain or into household bins. Research use only: no diagnostic claims, no human material, no live animals. This is a summary, not legal advice; check the current rules where you are.

Never bypassed, in code or in design

  • Lid interlock: opening the lid cuts the excitation LED and stops every motor (the steppers and the pumps) in hardware.
  • Moving parts: the stepper gears sit under fixed printed covers and the pumps' roller heads are enclosed, so fingers and hair can't be caught.
  • Leak cutoff: a leak trip cuts the pumps, any heater and the LED through a hardware latch that firmware can't clear.
  • Thermal cutoff: any heater gets a hardware cutoff rated ≤ 50 °C near printed parts. Version 1 has no heater on the stage at all.
  • No override: software never overrides these; there are no "force" flags.
  • Low voltage: ≤ 24 V DC inside, from certified external supplies with fused rails. Never wire mains.
  • Liquids: only the sealed sample vessel sits over the objective, with a drip ring, a sleeve and a leak sensor; no reservoirs above the optics or electronics.
  • Stage rules: no CMR liquids, no gel casting and no 95 °C steps on the stage.
  • Light: the excitation light, an LED behind a diffuser, must be in IEC 62471's Exempt or Risk Group 1 ("low risk") group as fitted, by its maker's rating or a measurement: X-WOW's rule is no light bright enough to hurt eyes looked at directly. A bare high-power blue LED can be Risk Group 2: the ams-OSRAM application note AN002 (2025-09-16, read 2026-10-12) gives a 470 nm blue LED of 5 cd as Risk Group 2. The light runs only inside the closed box, and opening the lid cuts it. Never look into the excitation light. The white and oblique LEDs, which the interlock doesn't cover, must be in IEC 62471's Exempt group, by their makers' rating. No lasers and no ultraviolet source in the reference build. The laser pointer in the family experiment is separate from the instrument and has its own safety box. Open: no LED is chosen yet, and whether a light this low-risk is bright enough for SWELL's fluorescence imaging hasn't been worked out.

Words on this page

A short glossary

Sarcomere
One repeating unit of a muscle fibre, about 1.69 µm long in broiler chicken breast (one published study). A muscle contracts by shortening them.
Myofibril
A thin thread inside a muscle cell, made of sarcomeres joined end to end. SWELL uses them as rulers.
Z-disc
The thin line where one sarcomere meets the next.
Expansion factor (E)
How many times bigger the gel made the sample: a length after expansion divided by the same length before.
Eax and Etr
The expansion factor along a myofibril (axial) and across it (transverse).
Anisotropy
Growing more in one direction than another. On this page it is Eax ÷ Etr − 1.
Non-affine
A distortion that no single stretch, shift and turn of the whole gel can explain, such as a tear or a fold.
PSF
Point spread function: the blurred spot a microscope makes of a single point of light. The narrower it is, the finer the detail you can see.
Nyquist sampling
Taking at least two pixels across the finest detail you want to see. With fewer, that detail is lost.
Strehl ratio
How bright the peak of a focused spot is compared with a perfect lens's, from 0 to 1; 1 means no aberration. On this page it is computed in simulation, relative to the gel's surface.
q
The number the label-free stripe test hangs on: stripe contrast × √(photons per sample). The higher q is, the more precisely the stripe spacing can be read.
DPC
Differential phase contrast: lighting a clear specimen from one side, then the other, and comparing the two images, so thin transparent structures show up without a stain.
Clock tile
One patch of the gel imaged again every 50 tiles during a scan, so that slow swelling during the scan can be measured and taken out.
Overview camera
A small camera that photographs the whole gel, to measure its overall size before and after expansion.
CMR
Carcinogenic, mutagenic or toxic for reproduction: hazard classes some gel chemicals carry. UK law restricts selling them to the public.
Simulated, synthetic
Made by a computer from a model, not measured on anything real.
