The numbers, honestly

What the simulation says, including where it fails

Every result here comes from synthetic gels with a known truth. Synthetic data tests the analysis pipeline against our own assumptions, not the chemistry: a real build would have to repeat every check on real gels.

Read this first. This section is version 1, measured on gels from our own generator. The only test built by a separate AI agent's generator, red-team gel S3 (see Tested harder), went worse: per-myofibril axial error 1.8% RMS with 7.5% beyond ±2%, a transverse bias of −17.8%, and 0% of the under-expanded patch flagged. Treat everything below as development results.

The first estimate was too optimistic, and the simulation said so

simulated

The first desk calculation said one myofibril segment of 10 sarcomeres gives its expansion to 0.21–0.35% (RMS), for assumed before-image contrast of 5–50%. It assumed perfectly regular sarcomeres. Real ones vary by about 3% from one to the next, and under that jitter (and at some sarcomere lengths) the original estimator (a free-frequency fit) locked onto side-lobes: 10–17% of myofibrils came out wrong by 7–19%. The first full whole-gel simulation failed 15 checks.

The fix is the Z-disc ladder: the Z-discs in the expanded image define a sarcomere-by-sarcomere coordinate, and the before-image is fitted on that ladder, so the jitter is carried along instead of fought. Segments need at least 8 sarcomeres. Result: 0.3–0.6% RMS per myofibril across simulation runs, 0.32–0.48% across the four gels of the final run and 0.35% on the blind gel, with at most 0.48% of myofibrils off by more than ±2%.

These precision figures hold only under the simulation's conditions: before-image stripes with a fundamental contrast of 10.5% at 5,000 detected photons per sample (q ≈ 7.4), shot noise only, a Gaussian blur of exactly known width, and one-dimensional profiles handed straight to the ruler, with no image to segment or stitch. The design's DPC before-image is predicted to reach only 2.3% contrast, made up for by about 200,000 detected photons per sample (q ≈ 10), again assuming shot noise only.

The error bar went up, and the simulation itself raised it.

Our own blind gel (version 1, development)

Gel S1b was our own blind test: the same generator with a new random seed, every planted defect moved and resized, and nothing tuned after it ran. It is not independent, because our generator shares our assumptions; red-team gel S3 is the red-team test. It holds 1,200 myofibrils in a 1.5 mm punch expanded 4.5×.

The two misses, plainly: a mild +2.5% stretch is caught under the wrong label, and a 1.5 µm tear is too small to see. Across all four synthetic gels, 31 of 33 checks pass, 1 fails and 1 is below the detection limit. Three of the gels (S0, S1, S2) are development gels, because the detectors were fixed while looking at S1; S1b was our own blind gel, and S3 has since replaced it as the red-team test. On development gel S1, a 2 µm tear was flagged over 89% of its area.

Coverage: on our own gels (version 1), about half the myofibrils carry the axial ruler

Every myofibril gets the transverse (width) ruler and the landmark (position) ruler. Only segments of at least 8 sarcomeres get the axial period ratio: 665 of 1,200 (55%) in the blind gel and 626 of 1,200 (52%) in the development gels. In real meat this would depend on how gently the tissue is homogenised.

Transverse isotropy and the shape-aware ruler

A gel can swell evenly along a myofibril and unevenly across it. So the pass criteria include transverse isotropy, Eax ÷ Etr − 1 within ±3% (95% CI, n ≥ 200 myofibrils), and the axial and transverse factors are always reported separately. That interval counts only the scatter between myofibrils, not error they all share, and on our synthetic gels it has proved too narrow (Tested harder, point 3), so a real test would have to use whole gels as the unit; that test isn't designed yet. On the blind gel the transverse factor per myofibril is 0.00% ± 0.50% (bias ± SD).

The width ruler is the fragile one: it needs a model of each cross-section. A boxy cross-section fitted as an ellipse is off by −9.0% to −15.2%, depending on the before-image method. So the pipeline measures each myofibril's cross-section shape from the expanded z-stack, and the bias drops to ≤ 0.04%.

Blur calibration still matters. If the before-image blur is calibrated 5% too wide, the bias is 1.05–3.23%, so the first gate calibrates it to ±2%. At that target the worst bias in our own model is 1.09%. Red-team gel S3 showed the model is not enough: a blur shape the ruler does not model biased it by −17.8%, with the right blur widths or not.

On development gel S2, a planted 2% anisotropy is recovered as −2.12% [−2.37%, −1.87%] (truth −2.03%), and a planted gel stretch of 2.00% along 23° is found by the orientation test as 2.03% along 23°.

open problem

Not enough light in the worst case

Widefield optics see the whole thickness of an expanded myofibril, so out-of-focus light adds a background pedestal. At the lowest labelling simulated (0.5% of amines) on a 2 µm myofibril, the signal-to-noise ratio falls 12.4 → 5.6 against a target of 10. Reaching the target needs 3.2× the dose (exposure × irradiance), which costs bleaching. Before the pedestal was modelled, the first pass had given 12–42 across the labelling range.

Not solved. The named routes were a dose budget, deconvolution and a dye titration.

Tested harder

A fresh AI review found the weak spots. Here is what changed.

On 9 October 2026 a fresh AI review session (another Claude instance that had not built anything here, not a person) reran the download: the fingerprint matched, all 11 tests passed and every number reproduced. Then it listed what the tests could not show. Each point below is fixed, or stated as an open problem with its numbers. The first version was withdrawn on 10 October 2026, because it carried the original brief's superseded home-lab plan.

1 · BLIND ≠ INDEPENDENT

A red team built a gel we never saw

The old blind gel came from the same generator, and the pipeline was handed the before/after pairing. Now a separate AI agent (another Claude instance, so it may share our blind spots), which read only the data format and never the detector code, built gel S3: lost fibrils, debris, a fold, a bubble, curved and bundled fibrils, tilted fibrils, expansion drifting during the scan, and its own blur and noise. The pipeline now matches the fibrils itself, and its result on S3 is published as the red team scored it.

simulated What failed: the transverse ruler read widths about a fifth too small on average, which made the anisotropy call wrong in size (the sign was right). The cause is not the blur calibration: rerun with the true blur widths, the bias is still −17.4%. It is a blur shape the ruler does not model, and the pipeline's own landmark ruler already disagreed with it by −19.7% while the call was still made. Fixed after S3 (a development fix, since S3 has been seen): no anisotropy call while the two rulers disagree by more than 3%. On S3 the call is now blocked. The ordinary under- and over-expanded patches, the curved fibrils and the tilted region went unflagged, and only about a quarter of the pairs got an axial ratio. The pairing, the debris rejection and the gel-level factor held. Like our own gels, S3 reached the pipeline as one set of profiles per object, cut out of the red team's images by its emulated image front-end, so SWELL's own code never segmented or stitched an image in these tests: read the pairing and debris scores as best cases for real images. The drift correction held too, but the slope in the table can't show it: the planted drift rose to +0.48% and fell back, so even with no correction its straight-line slope would have been only +0.07%/h, smaller than that slope's standard error. What shows it is that after the clock tile, the median error in each of six time bins stays within ±0.1%. The slope also leaves out 10 of its 320 myofibrils, those with errors beyond 5%; with them it reads −0.15%/h. The local expansion flag already uses the landmark ruler's area change as well as the axial ratio, but it fires only where the change, smoothed by a Gaussian of 45 µm standard deviation, exceeds 1%, or four standard errors where that is larger, and nothing in the under-expanded patch crossed that line. How a patch's size and strength decide whether it is caught has not been measured. These are open problems; S3 has now been seen, so each fix needs a new blind gel.

2 · THE BEFORE-IMAGE IS LOAD-BEARING

A go/no-go for the stripes

simulated The axial ruler is only as good as the label-free stripes before expansion. Its error follows one number, q = striation contrast × √photons: go if q ≥ 1.2 (1% per myofibril at 10 sarcomeres). An uneven light that the old code ignored no longer seems to matter: with a 20% slope the per-myofibril error was 0.95% with the old code and is 0.28% now, against 0.34% and 0.39% with even light. Each figure is the RMS of its own 300 random draws, not shared between cases, with no confidence interval, so small differences mean little: the new code's lower figure with a slope than without one should not be read as a gain. predicted From the physics of each mode, the choice is differential phase contrast with four oblique LEDs (predicted contrast 2.3%, q ≈ 10, assuming about 200,000 detected photons per sample and shot noise only). In one acquisition, crossed polarisers lose fibrils near 0° and 90°: only 42%–78% of orientations reach the go line. A second acquisition with the polariser pair turned 45° was not simulated.

3 · A −0.1% BIAS

Broken ends, and effect sizes instead of "significant"

simulated The ladder used the Z-discs at the very ends of each fragment, which are cut in half, and that pulled the axial factor low. They are now ignored. The gel-mean error on our own five development gels is now −0.08% to 0.00%, and the per-myofibril error 0.15%–0.18% (under the simulated conditions listed in the numbers). "The confidence interval excludes zero" had called a perfect gel anisotropic; now anisotropy counts only beyond ±1%. On the perfect gel the estimate is −0.10% [−0.18%, −0.03%], called isotropic; a planted 2% anisotropy reads −2.16% [−2.44%, −1.81%] (truth −1.98%). The brackets are 95% confidence intervals from resampling myofibrils, which treats them as independent and leaves out error they all share, such as the blur and cross-section model, so they are too narrow: they miss the true value on 4 of our 5 own gels, and on S3. The ±1% rule also hides a small bias: on our own gels the transverse ruler reads +0.05% to +0.14% high. A corrected interval (the bias removed, a term for shared error, its coverage checked by simulation, whole gels as the unit) is an open problem.

4 · WHEN THE BEFORE-IMAGE IS TAKEN

After the gel sets, and the gap measured

untested A protocol decision, never run. The protocol took the before-image before anchoring and gelling, so any change in sarcomere length in between would read as an expansion error. The reference image is now taken after the gel sets, on the rinsed gel, and the first three gels are imaged before anchoring too, to measure what that step does. The rinse is not settled either: the plan rinses the gel in PBS, a salt solution, and a sodium-acrylate gel may start to swell or buckle in it (an AI review's reasoning, not a measurement), so the first gels must also show that the rinse leaves the gel's size unchanged, or the rinse must move to a liquid that does.

5 · ENOUGH LIGHT

How much dye the signal needs

For a signal-to-noise ratio of 10 at today's exposure, 0.81%–1.58% of the protein's amines must carry a dye (0.41%–0.79% at 200 ms). A pan-ExM-strength stain is estimated to label about 1.4% (range 0.30%–9.6%); the original recipe's weaker stain, about 0.14% (range 0.030%–0.96%), probably falls short: only the top of its range reaches what is needed. The model treats every amine in the protein as free to take a dye, but the anchoring step is expected to use some amines first, which it leaves out, so the real share could be lower. So the first gels titrate stronger stains. untested Still open until real gels measure it.

6 · THE GEL KEEPS MOVING

A clock tile for scan-time drift

simulated A gel can keep swelling during a two-hour scan, which would paint a fake gradient in the order the tiles were taken. One tile is now re-imaged every 50 tiles as a clock. On a synthetic gel drifting by 0.5%, the error trend falls from 0.22%/h to −0.036%/h (straight-line slopes, without standard errors: none were computed for this gel).

Still failing: on the blind gel, the gently stretched patch is flagged mostly by the wrong detector (40% by its own, 92% by any), and a 1.5 µm tear stays below the detection limit. Both are listed as open problems.

A blind test written by the same hand is a rehearsal. S3 is the first exam set by an agent that never saw the answers, though it was another AI instance, not a stranger.
