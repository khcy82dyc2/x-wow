Microscopy moved

SWELL

An expansion microscope designed to check every gel against chicken muscle.

A hydrogel swells the specimen 4 to 10 times so a microscope with a 3D-printed stage, designed to be cheap, could resolve detail below the light's limit, in the specimen's own units. Chicken myofibrils expanded alongside carry sarcomeres, built-in rulers that would report how much each fibril grew; whether that matches the gel around them is untested. It is a partial desk design (5 of its 9 design documents are written; the wet-lab protocol, the safety pack, the mechanical CAD and the instrument software are not), with simulations and a red-team test, given away free; it has never been built.

≥ £214 for the parts priced so far; most aren't priced yet, so there is no full cost

Sparks (10)

Ideas that passed the first test: each could make something hidden visible. Untested, not claims: none is called new before its search for earlier work, and none has a bar until it is a card.

  • spark · 2026-10-11 new Prior art: not yet searched

    Heat-map paper

    A liquid-crystal sheet changes colour with temperature, so it paints heat as a picture: a fading handprint, or a leaf kept cool by its own evaporation.

    Why it might matter, and where it came from

    A thirsty plant may show up warmer before it droops, so a cheap sheet could let anyone see a plant's thirst early.

    From: the owner's idea; the thirsty-plant use came from a headline in the HardwareX journal feed (the paper not yet opened). Earlier work: not yet searched.

  • spark · 2026-10-11 new Prior art: not yet searched

    Leaf-glow clock

    A leaf kept in the dark and then lit gives off a faint red glow that rises and falls over seconds to minutes as photosynthesis starts up (the Kautsky effect).

    Why it might matter, and where it came from

    A filtered phone camera might watch that curve, a clock of the leaf's own machinery, with ordinary visible light only.

    From: the owner's idea. Earlier work: not yet searched (the Kautsky effect is classic plant science; we will credit it).

  • spark · 2026-10-11 new Prior art: not yet searched

    Moiré magnifier

    Lay one printed line pattern over another and a movement too small to see becomes broad bands sweeping across the sheet.

    Why it might matter, and where it came from

    Two printed sheets might measure tiny bending or swelling, with the bands doing the magnifying.

    From: the owner's idea. Earlier work: not yet searched (moiré methods are long established; we will credit them).

  • spark · 2026-10-11 new Prior art: not yet searched

    Rolling-shutter time microscope

    A phone camera reads its picture one row at a time, so a single photo of something fast (a plucked string, a spinning fan) holds a time-line in its stripes.

    Why it might matter, and where it came from

    It could measure speeds and frequencies far too fast for the eye with nothing but the phone in your pocket.

    From: the owner's idea. Earlier work: not yet searched (rolling-shutter measurement is studied in research; we will credit what we find).

  • spark · 2026-10-11 new Prior art: not yet searched

    Screen-polariser microscope

    Many phone and laptop screens give out polarised light; with a second polarising film, raw starch grains glow with dark crosses that fade once the starch is cooked.

    Why it might matter, and where it came from

    It would show cooking happen at the level of how molecules are packed, using a screen you already own. Raw and already-cooked food side by side; any heating is an adult's job.

    From: the owner's idea. Earlier work: not yet searched.

  • spark · 2026-10-11 new Prior art: not yet searched

    Seedling speckle growth

    Laser light scattered off a living seedling makes a grainy speckle that shimmers as cells move and grow. This is biospeckle, an established research method; the spark is only about making it cheap and safe.

    Why it might matter, and where it came from

    How fast the speckle changes could show growth a camera can't see directly. Only a sealed Class 1 or 2 laser product, handled by an adult and never pointed at anyone's eyes.

    From: the owner's idea. Earlier work: biospeckle research comes first; the search for cheap versions is not yet done.

  • spark · 2026-10-11 new Prior art: not yet searched

    Shadow gate

    Anything small that crosses a thin beam of light dims it for a moment; timing those dips tells how big and how fast it was.

    Why it might matter, and where it came from

    Falling drops, seeds or grains could be sized and timed with a cheap light sensor or a phone's slow-motion video.

    From: a headline in the HardwareX journal feed (the paper not yet opened). Earlier work: not yet searched (light gates are common in schools).

  • spark · 2026-10-11 new Prior art: not yet searched

    Swing scale for hidden mass

    Hang an object on three strings and time how it twists: the timing tells how its mass is spread around its middle.

    Why it might matter, and where it came from

    A phone timer could show whether the mass of something sealed sits near its middle or out at its edges, without opening it.

    From: a headline in the HardwareX journal feed (the paper not yet opened). Earlier work: not yet searched (the three-string pendulum is a textbook method; we will credit it).

  • spark · 2026-10-10 Prior art: not yet searched

    ReLens

    A printed adapter and a phone app could turn one retired microscope objective into a measuring microscope that checks itself against a ruler on the slide.

    Why it might matter, and where it came from

    Retired lab optics are often thrown away; one good lens could be a school's first calibrated microscope.

    From: Back to Shine. Earlier work: not yet searched (phone microscope adapters are common; anything new would have to be the self-check).

  • spark · 2026-10-10 Prior art: not yet searched

    SlideBox

    A phone, a printed stage and free hosting might turn any school's box of slides into an explorable SHRINK world.

    Why it might matter, and where it came from

    Whole-slide scanners are out of reach for most schools; their slide boxes sit in cupboards.

    From: X-WOW's slide-scanner archive and SHRINK. Earlier work: not yet searched (phone slide-scanning and open microscopes exist; we will credit them).

Killed, and why (3)

How the bar is counted

Each card says what the invention does, what it would cost and where it stands. A bar moves only when the proof file for that stage exists (a theory note with its numbers, a priced parts list, a build guide), and its date is the day that file first existed in X-WOW's own records, not the day it went online. The board grows when an idea passes X-WOW's gate: cheap, wow, unique, a seeing core. It is never padded. SWELL, the first invention, predates the gate and has not passed it; its card says why. Every card is free to build, change and claim. We claim nothing on any of them, but we can't promise that nobody else holds a patent: each invention's page says what we found. Below the cards are the Sparks: new ideas shown the day they pass the first test, before the deeper checks, and the ideas we killed, with the reason.

  1. Concept (10): a card with the hook, three sentences, the twelve-principle triage, the prior-art search and the AI reviewer's verdict.
  2. Theory checked (15): the science written out with at least three computed or cited numbers; the card names who checked it (so far, only AIs).
  3. Design drafted (15): drawings, a schematic or CAD files (a text document, a plot or a render never counts).
  4. Parts priced (10): at least five parts, each with a price, a link and the date checked.
  5. Build guide (15): numbered steps, a safety section and the built-in check.
  6. Prototype built (20): hardware, a build report from someone outside X-WOW, with who checked it (X-WOW itself stops at simulated prototypes); software, a public working version at its address.
  7. Validated (15): measured results against the claims, with who checked them, checked independently.

The bar adds every stage whose proof exists. The stage named on a card is the furthest one with every stage before it done, so a card never claims a step it skipped.
